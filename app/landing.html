Every denied claim
gets its recourse.

Recourse is an AI agent that works medical claim denials end to end: it reads the denial, the clinical note and the payer's policy, decides whether to correct and resubmit, appeal, write off or escalate, then does the paperwork across Gmail, Drive, Sheets, Calendar and Slack. A human approves anything irreversible.

Denials are the most persistent revenue leak in a clinic, and most are never worked.

Working a denial means reading the reason code, finding the note, checking the payer's policy, drafting a letter, tracking a deadline and updating a ledger. It takes a person 20 minutes per claim, so the long tail simply gets written off.

R

Rules decide the category. The model only reads.

A deterministic cascade handles everything that is arithmetic: duplicates, timely-filing maths, appeal windows, dollar thresholds, member-ID mismatches. The language model is called for exactly two reading tasks, and every rule that fires is shown on the card.

  • Does this clinical note meet this payer's policy criteria? Quotes required, confidence gated.
  • What is the one concrete fix for a rejected claim? "Can't tell" is an allowed answer.
  • Anything below the confidence bar goes to a physician, never to an automatic write-off.

✓

Acts across five apps. A human signs anything irreversible.

Recourse reads from Gmail, Drive and Sheets, and writes to all five. The plan goes to Slack first; the email leaves only after someone clicks approve.

  • Drive — the letter is saved to appeals/ before it is sent, so there is always a record.
  • Gmail — appeal or resubmission goes to the payer, from the practice's own inbox.
  • Calendar — appeal deadline booked with a reminder; urgent if under 14 days.
  • Sheets — the claim ledger flips status, written last so a failure can't lie.
  • Slack — approve/reject buttons, escalations to the physician, a daily summary.

Evaluated before it touched a real inbox.

Twenty seeded scenarios run against stateful replicas of the five apps, with fault injection, on a deterministic mock model and on the real one.

  • Judgement: appeal when criteria are met, refuse when they aren't, recognise an emergency exception.
  • Arithmetic: expired windows, late filing, duplicates, below-threshold dollars.
  • Safety: unknown claim, missing note, garbage mail, Gmail outage, rejected plan, double ingest.
  • One finding changed the design: the model got a member-ID fix wrong once in three runs, so that decision became a rule.

Why I built this

I build software for healthcare and clinical trials, and a while ago I worked on a medical coding assistant: a tool that reads a clinical note and suggests the ICD and CPT codes. It was good at the coding. What I didn't expect was where the work actually went afterwards. The claims left the practice, and a meaningful share of them came back denied, and then nothing happened. Not because the denials were right, but because working one takes a person twenty minutes of reading, looking things up, drafting and tracking, and there were always more urgent things than a $185 claim from July.

That is the part I couldn't leave alone. Roughly two-thirds of denied claims are never appealed or resubmitted, and about seventy percent of the ones that are appealed get paid. The money is there. The bottleneck is a triage task that is mostly rules with a small amount of reading in the middle. That shape is exactly what a well-built agent is good at: let rules decide the category and the arithmetic, let the model read a note against a policy, and let a human approve the parts that can't be undone.

Recourse is the version of that idea I could build in a day, on the tools a small practice already has. It is deliberately conservative. It would rather escalate than guess, and it was tested against twenty ways of going wrong before it sent a single real email.

— George A. Johny · Multi-App AI Agent Hackathon, September 2026

What happens when you open the worklist

A five-step tour runs the first time. Here it is in one glance.

1
A denial arrives

Poll the inbox, or ingest one of the seventeen fixture denials to see the agent work on demand.

2
Context is gathered

Claim, ledger row, patient record, clinical note and payer policy are pulled from Drive and Sheets.

3
Rules, then reading

The rule cascade decides the category. The model reads the note only when a judgement call is needed.

4
A plan, not an action

The card shows the decision, the rules that fired and the drafted letter. Nothing has been sent yet.

5
You approve

Approve here or in Slack. Then the letter is saved, sent, the deadline booked and the ledger updated.
